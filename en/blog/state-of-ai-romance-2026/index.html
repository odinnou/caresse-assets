The State of AI Romance 2026.

A data-driven look at how artificial intelligence, immersive audio and shifting expectations around privacy and personalization are reshaping modern intimacy — built entirely from public research, not internal survey data.

📅 Published August 7, 2026 · ⏱️ 22 min read · 📚 20+ cited sources

A note on methodology. Every statistic in this report is drawn from publicly published research — Pew Research Center, Edison Research, Cisco, McKinsey, Salesforce, Ofcom, Cigna, Forbes Health, EMARKETER and others — with direct source links. We did not run our own survey for this edition and we do not present internal product data as market data. Where figures come from market-forecast reports (rather than peer-reviewed studies), we say so explicitly. The full source list is at the bottom of this page.

Executive Summary

Five shifts define 2026.

Across dozens of independent studies published in the past eighteen months, five trends stand out clearly enough to organize a whole year of consumer behavior around. None of them are about any single app or company — they're structural shifts in how people adopt AI, consume audio, and think about their own privacy and time.

01 · Generative AI

Generative AI has moved from novelty to daily habit.

The story of AI adoption in 2026 isn't a single breakout moment — it's a steady, compounding curve. Pew Research's February 2026 survey found that 49% of US adults now use an AI chatbot, with a striking 24% reporting daily use. That's not experimentation; that's a habit embedded in a quarter of American adults' daily routine.

EMARKETER's modeling tells a similar story at a larger scale: an estimated 133.0 million people in the US used generative AI in 2026, roughly 39.2% of the population, up 9.8% from 121.1 million (35.8%) the year before. Globally, adoption reached an estimated 16.3% of the world's population by late 2025, with usage concentrated heavily among people already comfortable navigating digital services.

What's more telling than the topline number is what the usage is for. Pew's breakdown shows the emotional and personal use cases are still a minority of total chatbot use but are growing fastest: 10% of US adults use a chatbot for emotional support or advice, and 4% specifically for companionship. That's a small slice of a very large and growing pie — enough to matter, not enough to be the main story yet.

Sources: Pew Research Center, "Americans and AI 2026" · EMARKETER, 2026

02 · Immersive audio

Audio consumption is at an all-time high — and increasingly a private, at-home ritual.

While video has grabbed headlines in the podcast industry, the underlying audio habit has never been stronger. Edison Research's The Podcast Consumer 2026 found that 58% of Americans aged 12+ consume a podcast every month — an estimated 167 million people — with weekly consumption at 45% of the population, roughly 130 million individuals.

Zooming out to all digital audio (not just podcasts), 79% of the US population — about 228 million listeners — reports listening monthly, and 73% listen weekly. Daily podcast consumers now spend an average of 5 hours and 11 minutes per day with audio sources of any kind.

66%listen mostly at home

Two-thirds of weekly podcast consumers now listen most often at home, up from 61% in 2024 and 64% in 2025 — a steady multi-year climb toward audio as a private, domestic habit rather than a commute filler.

Source: Edison Research, "The Podcast Consumer 2026"

This matters for how we think about intimate and personal audio specifically. The infrastructure and habit of "putting in headphones alone, at home, and listening to something made for you" is no longer a niche behavior — it is, statistically, the default way the majority of the population already engages with audio content. The category most primed to benefit from that shift is content designed for individual, private listening rather than shared or ambient consumption.

Sources: Edison Research, "The Podcast Consumer 2026" · National Public Media / Edison, "Infinite Dial 2025"

03 · Modern couples

The real barrier to intimacy in 2026 is time, not desire.

A March 2026 Tawkify survey of 911 partnered American adults asked directly what gets in the way of intimacy. The answer wasn't a lack of attraction or connection — it was logistics. 65% of respondents named busy schedules as the most common barrier, well ahead of stress or anxiety (41%) and low libido (33%).

Despite that friction, overall satisfaction remains high: 82% of married or partnered adults report being satisfied with their relationship. The gap between "satisfied" and "intimate as often as we'd like" is largely a scheduling and initiation problem, not an emotional one. Separately, phone behavior is a measurable drag on connection: 46% of adults report experiencing "phubbing" (being ignored in favor of a partner's phone) in their relationship, and a 2025 meta-analysis linked partner phubbing to lower satisfaction and reduced intimacy.

Read together, the picture is a generation of couples who are fundamentally satisfied with their relationships but structurally short on the unscheduled time and low-friction rituals that intimacy used to run on.

Sources: Tawkify survey, 2026 · Connected Couples, "Relationship Statistics," 2026

04 · Personalization

Personalization stopped being a nice-to-have across every category consumers touch.

Outside of romance specifically, the broader consumer research on personalization is unambiguous. McKinsey finds that 71% of consumers now expect personalized interactions from the products and services they use, and 76% report frustration when that expectation isn't met. Salesforce's research puts a similar number on brand expectations: 73% of customers expect companies to understand their unique needs.

This isn't a marketing statistic in isolation — it reflects a broad recalibration of expectations that generative AI itself helped create. Once consumers experience software that adapts to specific, individual input (a name, a preference, a context) in one category, they carry that expectation into every other category, including the most personal ones. The bar for what counts as "generic" content keeps rising every year.

Sources: McKinsey, "The next frontier of personalized marketing" · Salesforce personalization research, via involve.me, 2026

05 · Privacy

Privacy became the price of admission for anything AI-powered.

As AI tools handle increasingly personal input, trust and privacy have moved from a compliance checkbox to a core product requirement. Cisco's 2026 Data and Privacy Benchmark Study found that AI is now the primary catalyst driving 90% of organizations to expand their privacy programs, with 93% planning further investment specifically to keep pace with AI-era complexity and rising customer expectations.

The direction of travel is consistent across every study we reviewed: as AI becomes more capable of using personal data to personalize an experience, users become more selective about which apps they'll trust with that data in the first place. For any product that asks for identity-adjacent input — a name, a preference, a voice — the ability to demonstrate anonymity or minimal data retention is increasingly a competitive differentiator, not just a legal requirement.

Source: Cisco Newsroom, "AI Fuels Surge in Data Privacy Investments," January 2026

06 · Dating app fatigue

Swipe fatigue is measurable, and it's reshaping how singles look for connection.

Perhaps the clearest behavioral signal in this year's research is a documented pullback from swipe-based dating apps. A Forbes Health survey (July 2025) found that nearly 4 in 5 dating app users report feeling emotionally, mentally, or physically burned out sometimes, often, or always. That echoes a broader 2024 finding that 78% of users felt similarly exhausted.

Cigna's 2025 survey found that 57% of Americans report feeling lonely, with a notable gap between single adults (39%) and married adults (22%). Researchers have also found that dating-app users report modestly worse psychological well-being on average than non-users, across measures including loneliness, anxiety and distress — correlation the studies are careful not to overstate as causation, but a signal worth watching.

The behavioral response is already visible: in-person singles events on Eventbrite roughly doubled between 2022 and 2025, with an 85% jump in attendance year-over-year at their 2024 peak. People aren't necessarily giving up on connection — many are actively looking for lower-pressure, less performative ways to find it.

Sources: ScienceBlog, dating app burnout research, 2025 · Axios, "Dating app fatigue," May 2026

07 · Looking ahead

What to watch in 2027.

Extrapolating cautiously from this year's data (and being explicit about what's a forecast versus a fact), three questions look most likely to define next year's edition of this report:

  • Does emotional and companionship use of AI keep growing as a share of total chatbot use, or plateau? Pew's 10%/4% figures for emotional support and companionship use are small today but have grown from a near-zero base; whether that curve keeps compounding or flattens out is one of the more consequential open questions in consumer AI.
  • Does the at-home audio shift continue? If weekly podcast listeners keep moving from commute to home (61% → 64% → 66% over three years), audio products built for private, individual listening — rather than background or shared listening — stand to benefit disproportionately.
  • Does privacy-by-design become a marketing claim companies compete on? With 95% of organizations already calling privacy essential to AI trust, 2027 is likely the year "no account required" and "anonymous by default" become standard claims across far more categories than they are today, not just security-conscious ones.

We'll revisit each of these directly in the State of AI Romance 2027 edition, with a "What changed since last year" section comparing this year's baseline numbers to the following year's.

Conclusion

What these trends mean, taken together.

None of these six trends exist in isolation, and none of them are about any single product. But laid side by side, they describe a fairly specific set of conditions: more people comfortable with AI-generated content than ever before; audio listening at record highs and increasingly a private, at-home habit rather than a commute filler; couples who are satisfied with their relationships but structurally short on time and low-friction rituals; a near-universal consumer expectation that digital experiences adapt to the individual rather than staying generic; privacy treated as a prerequisite for trust rather than an afterthought; and a documented, broad-based fatigue with high-pressure, performative ways of finding connection.

That combination — AI-generated, personal, audio-first, privacy-respecting, low-pressure — is the design space Caresse operates in. Frequently asked questions

State of AI Romance 2026 — your questions.

How many people use generative AI in 2026?

EMARKETER estimates 133.0 million people used generative AI in the US in 2026, about 39.2% of the population, up from 121.1 million (35.8%) in 2025. Pew Research found 49% of US adults report using an AI chatbot, with 24% using one daily as of its February 2026 survey.

Is podcast and audio listening still growing in 2026?

Yes. Edison Research's The Podcast Consumer 2026 found 58% of Americans 12+ consume a podcast monthly (about 167 million people), and 79% of the US population listens to digital audio monthly. Two-thirds of weekly podcast consumers now listen most often at home, up from 61% in 2024.

Do consumers actually expect personalized digital experiences?

Yes. McKinsey reports 71% of consumers expect personalized interactions and 76% get frustrated when they don't happen. Salesforce separately found 73% of customers expect companies to understand their unique needs and expectations.

Has data privacy become more important to consumers because of AI?

Cisco's 2026 Data and Privacy Benchmark Study found AI is the primary catalyst driving 90% of organizations to expand their privacy programs, and 95% of organizations now say privacy is essential to building customer trust in AI-powered services.

Are people getting tired of dating apps?

Signs point that way. A Forbes Health survey found nearly 4 in 5 dating app users report feeling burned out sometimes, often, or always. Separately, 65% of dating apps downloaded in 2024 were deleted within a month, and in-person singles events on Eventbrite roughly doubled between 2022 and 2025.

What is the single biggest barrier to intimacy for couples today?

Time, not desire. A 2026 Tawkify survey of partnered American adults found busy schedules are the most commonly cited barrier to intimacy, reported by 65% of respondents, ahead of stress or anxiety (41%) and low libido (33%).
